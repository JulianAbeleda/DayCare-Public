# Truncation, not verbosity, is the real failure

RL post-training on reasoning models reliably makes responses longer — DeepSeek-R1-Zero's average length rose from under 1,000 to over 14,000 tokens over training ([DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)) — and the literature now largely agrees this growth is at least partly an **optimization artifact of GRPO/PPO's length-dependent normalization**, not purely emergent reasoning skill: Dr. GRPO shows the standard objective divides by response length in a way that under-penalizes long wrong answers ([arXiv:2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)), and DAPO shows sample-level (vs. token-level) loss aggregation lets long, low-quality rollouts escape gradient pressure, driving entropy and length to blow up together ([arXiv:2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)). Separately, and more directly relevant to the applied case here, DAPO explicitly diagnoses that **scoring truncated/overlong samples identically to wrong answers injects reward noise that "confuses the model regarding the validity of its reasoning process"** ([arXiv:2503.14476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)) — this is close to a direct description of the reported symptom (truncated turns doubling from 7.3% to 15.1%, blank answers rising from 60 to 95 of 712). No source in the reviewed literature treats truncation as *better* than a wrong-but-complete answer; the field's convergent answer is to either exclude truncated samples from the loss entirely (DAPO's Overlong Filtering, SimpleTIR's trajectory-level exclusion of "void turns") or to penalize truncation on a graduated scale that is *at least as bad as, and near the cap approaches worse than, an ordinary wrong answer* — never to keep it exactly equal to "wrong" once training shows instability, and never to reward it. For the specific applied setup — RLOO with binary outcome reward on a 4B thinking-on hybrid model, where cap-hits doubled and blank post-tool-call answers rose sharply while accuracy also rose — report-writer inference (not directly evidenced by any single source) is that the accuracy gain and the truncation/blank-answer regression are two faces of the same underlying dynamic: the model is learning to spend more tokens deliberating after a rejected tool call because that behavior is rewarded when it fits under budget, but under an outcome-only, length-blind reward there is no differential pressure between "ran long and got it right" and "ran long, got cut off, and produced nothing," so probability mass drifts toward longer generations with a growing tail that overflows the cap.

## Why RL inflates length: two distinct, additive normalization biases

The base phenomenon is well established and reproduced across independent replications (SimpleRL-Zero, Open-Reasoner-Zero, PRIME-Zero), which the field treats as evidence the length-growth curve is a property of the *algorithm class* (GRPO/PPO-style RL on reasoning tasks) rather than one lab's idiosyncratic setup ([Dr. GRPO, arXiv:2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)). Dr. GRPO isolates two separable biases in the standard GRPO objective. The **response-level length bias** comes from dividing the per-sample loss by the token count |o_i|: for correct (positive-advantage) responses this gives stronger gradient updates to *shorter* correct answers (rewarding brevity when right), while for incorrect (negative-advantage) responses the same 1/|o_i| term shrinks the penalty for longer wrong answers — net effect, wrong answers drift longer over training ([arXiv:2503.20783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)). The **question-level difficulty bias** comes from dividing the centered reward by its group standard deviation, over-weighting near-degenerate (all-right or all-wrong) questions. Critically, Dr. GRPO finds this length-normalization bug is not confined to GRPO — it appears in the PPO loss code of trl, OpenRLHF, verl, SimpleRL-Zero, and Open-Reasoner-Zero as well, likely inherited from pretraining-era loss-normalization conventions ([arXiv:2503.20783, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)). DAPO's independent account targets the same symptom from the gradient-contribution side: GRPO's default sample-level loss (average within-sequence, then average across sequences) gives every sequence equal weight regardless of length, so individual tokens inside long sequences are under-trained, letting long repetitive/gibberish rollouts escape correction — this is what DAPO's own naive-GRPO baseline showed as length spiking to ~5,000 tokens alongside entropy collapse, before a fix ([arXiv:2503.14476, Sec. 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)). The two mechanisms are complementary — advantage-reweighting (Dr. GRPO) and per-token gradient magnitude (DAPO) — but both implicate length-dependent normalization as the root cause, and both fixes (removing the normalization, switching to token-level loss) are reported to *reduce* length growth while holding or improving accuracy, which is the strongest evidence in this literature that length growth is separable from accuracy gains rather than a necessary cost of them. Dr. GRPO's controlled 1.5B ablation (same data/template, GRPO vs. Dr. GRPO) shows incorrect-response length diverging sharply under GRPO (~1,800 tokens) versus staying flat under Dr. GRPO (~1,000–1,200 tokens) with similar-or-better benchmark accuracy ([arXiv:2503.20783, Fig. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)) — direct evidence that fixing the normalization curbs exactly the "wrong answers get longer" pathology, not overall reasoning length. One gap worth flagging: none of the reviewed sources directly audits RLOO's loss-normalization behavior for this same bias, though a separate paper (Arora & Zanette) reports observing length reduction in an RLOO setup even with its length-penalty coefficient set to zero, which they trace to RLOO's loss being normalized by response length — an implementation-level confound structurally identical to the GRPO/PPO bug ([arXiv:2502.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04463)). This is report-writer-flagged as the single most directly relevant data point to the applied case, since RLOO is the algorithm in use.

## DAPO's overlong handling is the closest published analogue to the reported symptom

DAPO treats truncation as a distinct problem from length growth in general, and proposes two techniques used together, not as alternatives. **Overlong Filtering** masks the loss of truncated samples entirely — no gradient contribution at all — and is reported to "significantly stabilize training and enhance performance," with an ablation (Figure 5) showing generation entropy exploding to ~5 nats without it versus staying flat (~0–0.5 nats) with it ([arXiv:2503.14476, Sec. 3.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)). **Soft Overlong Punishment** (Eq. 13) is layered on top: zero penalty until the response enters a defined buffer window before the hard cap, then a linearly increasing penalty (`((L_max − L_cache) − |y|) / L_cache`) as it approaches the cap, saturating at −1 at the hard cutoff. DAPO's concrete hyperparameters were L_max = 20,480, with a 4,096-token soft-punish buffer inside that (target length 16,384) ([arXiv:2503.14476, Eq. 13, Sec. 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)). Progressive ablation on AIME24 avg@32 shows overlong filtering alone scoring 36, and adding soft overlong punishment on top raising it to 41 ([DAPO ablation](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476v1)) — the two are additive, sequential interventions, not competing designs. DAPO's own stated rationale directly matches the applied problem: assigning truncated samples the same punitive reward as wrong answers "may introduce noise... as a sound reasoning process can be penalized solely due to its excessive length," which "can potentially confuse the model regarding the validity of its reasoning process" ([arXiv:2503.14476, Sec. 3.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476)). Complementary to reward shaping, curriculum-style context lengthening — training first at a short cap and widening it over stages — is reported by DeepScaleR (train at 8K, then 16K, then 24K, reaching 43.3% on AIME2024 at a fraction of the compute of full-length training from the start; note DeepScaleR appears to lack a formal arXiv listing, so FastCuRL, [arXiv:2503.17287](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17287), is the arXiv-indexed citation for the same curriculum-length-cap technique) as a separate, complementary lever from reward shaping — it changes what the cap *is* over training rather than how truncation is scored at a fixed cap.

## Length-aware rewards work but need dynamic targets, decoupling from correctness, and warmup

A second family of methods, distinct from truncation-handling, targets the general "outputs are longer than they need to be" problem via explicit length-aware rewards, and every method reports the same qualitative shape: large length cuts (40–80%) are achievable at 0–5% accuracy cost, but the size of the free cut shrinks sharply as problem difficulty rises, and every method reports an accuracy "cliff" past some penalty strength. Kimi k1.5 uses a length reward normalized against the min/max length within the same rollout group — correct-and-shorter gets up to +0.5, incorrect-and-long is penalized, incorrect-and-short is roughly neutral — and explicitly warns that "length penalty may slow down training during the initial phases," so they run standard RL first and switch on the length penalty only afterward, reaching 60.8 on AIME2024 at ~3,272 tokens average ([arXiv:2501.12599](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12599)). ShorterBetter computes a per-group dynamic length target (the shortest *correct* response in the sampled group, or the group mean if none is correct) and — critically — applies the length penalty **regardless of correctness**, specifically so incorrect responses can't dodge the length constraint by being short; on 1.5B in-domain math this achieves up to 80% length reduction with accuracy actually improving slightly, though larger penalty coefficients risk "length hacking" that degrades accuracy, and out-of-domain accuracy still drops 1–2% even at conservative settings ([arXiv:2504.21370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21370)). L1/LCPO instead conditions the reward and prompt on an explicit target length, giving precise promptable control in-domain (~3% length error) but degrading badly out-of-domain (20–40% error on MMLU) — and notably, training the model to hit length targets very precisely costs it flexibility to run long on genuinely hard problems, costing ~1% accuracy concentrated on AIME ([arXiv:2503.04697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04697)). "Training Language Models to Reason Efficiently" normalizes the length signal per-prompt (against the mean/std of lengths sampled for that specific prompt) precisely to avoid punishing long CoT on genuinely hard problems, and reports the achievable free length cut shrinking from 65% (GSM8K) to 37% (MATH500) to just 16% (AIME2024) as difficulty rises, with accuracy collapsing much faster than length at high penalty strength ([arXiv:2502.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04463)). ThinkPrune instead uses a hard, non-continuous gate — truncate at a cap and score correctness on the truncated output — combined with an iterative, progressively-tightening schedule of caps across training rounds rather than one fixed target; this beats one-shot aggressive pruning and achieves a 65% length reduction with a slight accuracy *gain* on an under-saturated 1.5B model, though more RL-saturated models (QwQ-32B) show a sharp, non-gradual collapse past a critical pruning threshold ([arXiv:2504.01296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01296)). "Demystifying Long Chain-of-Thought Reasoning" documents the underlying instability these methods are all responding to — CoT length can grow unstably until it hits the context window, at which point truncation itself causes accuracy to decline — and proposes a piecewise cosine reward enforcing correct > incorrect, and among correct responses shorter > longer, but *also* enforcing that among incorrect responses, shorter is penalized **more** than longer, specifically to stop the model from learning to bail out early to dodge a length penalty; they also note length rewards eventually get hacked via repetition given enough compute, requiring an explicit repetition penalty as a companion term ([arXiv:2502.03373](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03373)). Two adjacent methods use different training paradigms rather than RL reward-shaping: s1's "budget forcing" is a pure test-time/inference control (forcibly truncating or extending via appended "Wait" tokens after SFT only, no RL) which L1's RL-based approach is reported to outperform at matched budgets ([s1, arXiv:2501.19393](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19393); [L1, arXiv:2503.04697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04697)); and the "Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=?" overthinking paper uses SimPO/DPO-style preference optimization rather than online RL, diagnosing redundant re-derivation on easy problems and achieving ~45% length reduction with roughly flat accuracy on easier benchmarks but a 3.4-point drop on AIME24 ([arXiv:2412.21187](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21187)). Across this whole family, the converging design pattern is: use a dynamic, per-prompt-or-per-group length target rather than one fixed global number; decouple the length term additively from the correctness term so wrong-and-short answers can't escape it; shape the penalty as soft/graduated or on a receding schedule rather than switching it on at full strength from step one; and expect the accuracy cost to be non-linear — flat, then a cliff — rather than smoothly proportional to the length cut.

## Tool-use RL: outcome-only reward degenerates into gradient explosions and blank turns, not documented length blow-ups

The tool-use literature does not report the specific symptom "reasoning gets longer after a tool-call result" as a named phenomenon in the base papers — ToRL and ReTool both use plain +1/−1 outcome-only rewards and report predominantly positive emergent behavior (cross-validation, self-correction), with ReTool reporting response length *decreasing* ~40% as an emergent side effect, not something explicitly shaped ([ToRL, arXiv:2503.23383](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23383); [ReTool, arXiv:2504.11536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11536)). Neither paper's fetched content specifies how truncated/overlong tool-use rollouts are scored — this is a direct gap against the applied question. The mechanistically closest match to the reported symptom (blank answers spiking after tool results) is **SimpleTIR**'s "void turn" diagnosis: under multi-turn tool-integrated RL, tool feedback that deviates from the model's own training distribution causes subsequent generation to become highly stochastic, producing collapsed, low-probability, nonsensical or empty continuations; these low-probability tokens then trigger unbounded importance-sampling ratios and catastrophic gradient-norm explosions that destabilize training ([arXiv:2509.02479](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02479)). SimpleTIR also documents a related credit-assignment failure specific to sparse outcome-only reward in multi-turn settings: a trajectory that fails on a *later* turn receives a negative terminal reward that unfairly penalizes good earlier reasoning, pushing the policy to collapse toward safer, shorter, single-turn generations — a distinct but related pathology to the blank-answer/truncation problem. SimpleTIR's fix is trajectory-level exclusion — filtering out entire rollouts containing a void turn from the policy update, rather than token-level masking or reward shaping — which the paper reports "prevents harmful gradient backpropagation" and fixes the credit-assignment problem by not penalizing successful early turns for a later collapse. This is structurally the tool-use analogue of DAPO's Overlong Filtering (exclude the degenerate sample from the loss rather than score it), but keyed on a void-turn detector rather than a length-cap detector. **DeSA** documents a related but distinct set of outcome-only-reward failure modes in search agents — no-search, duplicate queries, invalid searches — and fixes them with a two-stage curriculum that first optimizes a recall-based search-quality reward, then optimizes exact-match answer reward, rather than relying on one outcome-only signal to shape both behaviors; this improved a 3B model's deficient-search rate from 23.4% to 7.0% and accuracy by 8.0 points averaged across seven benchmarks ([arXiv:2510.04695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04695)). DeSA's failure modes are about *whether/how often* the tool is invoked, not blank-answer generation specifically, so it is a partial rather than direct match. Taken together, report-writer inference: the tool-use RL literature's answer to "what happens under outcome-only reward" is convergent with the general-RL literature's answer to "what happens with truncation" — both converge on excluding clearly-degenerate samples (void turns, overlong truncations) from the gradient entirely as the stabilizing first move, with graduated penalties layered back in once training is stable, rather than scoring the degenerate case identically to (or better than) an ordinary wrong answer.

## Recommendations for the RLOO / Nemotron 3 Nano post-tool-result setting

The reported symptom — cap-hit rate doubling (7.3%→15.1%), blank post-tool-call answers rising (60→95 of 712), while accuracy rose overall (+7 points) and sharply on the rejected-calculator-recovery category (+16 points), with truncated turns currently scored identically to wrong answers (0) — maps closely onto the DAPO diagnosis (naive truncation-as-wrong reward "confuses the model regarding the validity of its reasoning process," Sec. 3.4) and the Dr. GRPO/Arora-Zanette mechanism (length-normalized loss under-penalizes long trajectories, and this bias is specifically confirmed to exist in RLOO implementations even without any explicit length penalty). The following ranks the six options against the literature; all effect-size estimates for *this* setup are report-writer inference, since no source runs an RLOO/4B/tool-recovery experiment identical to this one.

**(a) Truncated scores below wrong (e.g., −0.5)** — no source directly evidences a flat, fixed "worse-than-wrong" constant; DAPO's closest analogue is graduated, not flat (see e below). Making truncation strictly worse than wrong-non-empty answers correctly signals "this is worse than a clean miss" and directly targets the blank-answer symptom, but on its own does nothing to fix the mechanism that's driving trajectories toward the cap in the first place — it only changes the terminal reward magnitude, not the pressure that produces the long tail. Expected effect (inference): likely reduces cap-hit rate somewhat by making truncation a harder-penalized target, but risks teaching the model to bail out with a short, plausible-looking wrong answer rather than continuing to reason after a rejected tool call — the same failure mode "Demystifying" explicitly designed around (penalizing short-wrong more than long-wrong, precisely the opposite asymmetry). Recommend only as one *component*, not standalone, and paired with (d)/(e) below.

**(b) Per-token length penalty** — well-evidenced family (Kimi k1.5, ShorterBetter, Arora & Zanette, L1) but every source that reports it also reports it needs warmup/staging (Kimi k1.5: penalty from step 1 "may slow down training") and a dynamic (per-group or per-prompt) target rather than a fixed one, because a flat target penalizes hard problems (here: post-rejection recovery, which plausibly needs more tokens) unfairly — L1 and ShorterBetter's core motivating design choice. Expected effect: could reduce the long tail, but on a narrow, tool-recovery-specific distribution with only 712 turns, learning a stable per-group dynamic target is exactly the kind of small-sample, high-variance setting where these methods' reported cliffs (accuracy collapsing disproportionately past some penalty strength) are hardest to tune around safely. Rank this below (c)/(d)/(e) for this specific case due to tuning risk and lack of any tool-use-specific validation of per-token length penalties in the reviewed literature.

**(c) Lower training cap** — no source directly proposes simply lowering the cap as a fix for truncation-driven degeneration; the evidenced analogue is the *opposite* direction — DeepScaleR/FastCuRL's curriculum widens the cap over training stages, starting low and raising it, rather than tightening a cap that's already causing truncation. Lowering the cap further while cap-hits are already rising would mechanically convert more borderline-long-but-correct trajectories into truncated (0-reward) ones, amplifying rather than fixing the reported symptom. Rank this lowest — it treats the visible symptom (cap hits) by making the cap bind more often, which the literature's curriculum-lengthening practice argues against.

**(d) Overlong filtering / masking truncated samples from the loss** — this is the best-evidenced, most directly on-point intervention available. DAPO reports it "significantly stabilizes training and enhances performance" and prevents entropy blow-up (Figure 5 ablation), and SimpleTIR's structurally identical trajectory-level exclusion of "void turns" is reported to fix exactly the pattern described here — gradient-instability and collapsed/blank generation following tool feedback that's out-of-distribution relative to the model's own outputs. Both papers converge on excluding the degenerate sample from the gradient as the *first* stabilizing move, before any graduated penalty is layered on. Expected effect (inference, extrapolated from DAPO/SimpleTIR): should directly stop truncated/blank rollouts from teaching the model "longer is fine even when it produces nothing," which is very plausibly a large piece of both the cap-hit and blank-answer increases. Rank this **first**.

**(e) Loss normalization fixes (token-level vs. sequence-level, Dr. GRPO-style)** — this is the mechanism-level fix rather than a reward-shaping patch, and it is the one most directly implicated by the specific algorithm in use: Arora & Zanette explicitly report RLOO's own loss normalization produces length effects even with an explicit length penalty *disabled*, meaning the current RLOO setup plausibly already carries the same length-bias bug Dr. GRPO documents for GRPO/PPO. If RLOO's implementation divides the loss by response length per-sample (the same bug pattern Dr. GRPO found across trl/OpenRLHF/verl), it directly explains why *incorrect or truncated* long trajectories are under-penalized relative to correct short ones — precisely the observed pattern (truncated turns effectively getting a "free pass" at 0 reward with weak gradient, same as an ordinary wrong answer, while genuinely long correct recoveries also get diluted gradient). Rank this **second**, tied closely with (d): it is a one-time implementation audit/fix (check whether the RLOO loss divides by |o_i| per-sample; if so, replace with a constant-normalization masked-mean per Dr. GRPO) rather than a new reward term, is unlikely to interact badly with the existing outcome reward, and directly targets the root cause DAPO and Dr. GRPO both attribute the length/instability coupling to.

**(f) Anything else** — the single most targeted intervention not on the enumerated list, following the Demystifying/DAPO/ShorterBetter pattern, is a **soft graduated truncation penalty inside a buffer window before the hard cap** (DAPO's Eq. 13 shape: 0 penalty until within N tokens of the cap, then linearly worsening to a floor at the cap), decoupled additively from the correctness reward. This directly answers the applied question more precisely than a flat "-0.5 for truncated" because it (i) doesn't punish trajectories that finish comfortably under budget, (ii) doesn't create the DAPO-documented "reward noise" of flatly punishing a sound-but-long reasoning process, and (iii) gives the model a smooth gradient toward finishing inside budget rather than a step function at the boundary. Given DAPO's own ablation (overlong filtering alone: 36 avg@32; filtering + soft punishment: 41), this is best treated as *additive to* (d), not a replacement.

**Recommended combination**: (d) overlong filtering/masking as the first move — mask the loss contribution of samples that hit the 4096-token cap (or are void/blank-after-tool-result per a SimpleTIR-style detector) so they stop injecting the "confusing" reward-noise signal DAPO identifies; in parallel, (e) audit and fix the RLOO loss normalization to remove any per-sample 1/|o_i| length division, per Dr. GRPO, since this is the most likely single mechanism behind both the doubled cap-hit rate and the fact that truncation currently scores identically (and with muted gradient) to an ordinary wrong answer; once training is observably stabilized under (d)+(e), layer on (f) a soft graduated within-buffer truncation penalty (DAPO Eq. 13 shape, a few hundred tokens of buffer before the 4096 cap) so trajectories that are trending toward the cap get a smooth corrective signal rather than a step function, while a hard −1 (worse than a merely-wrong-but-complete answer) applies only at the actual cap boundary. Do not adopt (a) as a standalone fix — the literature's repeated warning (Demystifying, ShorterBetter) is that penalizing "wrong" harder without penalizing "short-and-wrong" comparably risks teaching bail-out behavior, which is the opposite of what's wanted for the rejected-calculator-recovery category, where the +16-point gain suggests the model is learning something real by reasoning longer after rejection. Deprioritize (b) per-token length penalty and (c) lowering the cap for this iteration — both carry documented risks (tuning-sensitive collapse; mechanically increasing truncation) that are avoidable given that (d) and (e) target the actual reported mechanism more directly and with stronger, more analogous evidence.

## Conclusion

The literature's clearest, most load-bearing point for this case is one the applied setup's own numbers already suggest: length growth under outcome-only RL and truncation-driven degeneration are not two separate problems requiring two separate literatures — they are the same length-normalization pathology viewed from two ends, and DAPO, Dr. GRPO, and SimpleTIR each independently arrive at "exclude the degenerate sample from the gradient" as the fix that has to come *before* any reward-shaping refinement, because scoring truncation as merely "wrong" (or even "worse than wrong") without first fixing the normalization or masking still leaves the underlying gradient-dilution bug intact. The novel implication for this report is that treating this as a reward-design problem alone (options a–d as reward tweaks) is likely to under-fix it if the RLOO loss itself already carries the length-bias bug Arora & Zanette flag as present even with length penalties disabled — meaning the fastest, most literature-supported diagnostic step is not choosing a reward shape at all, but first checking whether the RLOO implementation in use divides its loss by response length per-sample, since if so, that single fix (option e) may resolve a large share of both the cap-hit doubling and the blank-answer increase before any new reward term is even needed.
